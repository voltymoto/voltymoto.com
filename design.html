Volty / Design

Every part earns its place.

The U1 was not styled and then engineered. It was packaged, welded, wired, numbered, tooled, and ridden, in that order. This is that process, shown in the state it is actually in.

How We Decide

Four rules, held against a real vehicle.

These are the four commitments the company is built on. Below is what each one actually decided on this bike, including the things it removed.

Rule 01

Design with Purpose

Enforced as a definitive filter, not a marketing catchphrase.

On the U1, this principle acts as a strict veto, eliminating fairings, body-colored battery covers, and secondary displays. These parts increase costs and complexity without adding range, payload, or uptime. The exploded view shows only what passed the veto: frame, bay, deck, covers, and racks.

Rule 02

Focus on a need

One rider, drawn to scale, sits behind every dimension.

The package is sized for a working rider at the end of a 12-hour shift, not a showroom average. Seat height, bar reach, deck height, and loaded rear rack weight were tailored to them. Between two viable options, we chose the one that performed better at hour 11 over the one that looked better initially.

Rule 03

Solve a specific problem

Each component targets a specific operational challenge.

Swappable battery packs offset volatile energy costs, a hinged access bay keeps diagnostic delays under a minute, and bolt-on panels with universal fasteners allow fast local repairs without specialized service centers. Every element is intentionally designed to solve a clear rider pain point.

Rule 04

Be meaningful

The definitive filter applied post-engineering.

A vehicle gains lasting significance by improving operator economics while remaining preferred for personal travel. The U1 achieves this with a distinct silhouette, functional suspension travel, and a dual-occupant seat. Without these features, it becomes a purely utilitarian tool that generates revenue but is easily replaced by cheaper alternatives.

The four rules come from the Vision. This page is the record of what happened when we held a real vehicle against them.

The Process

These seven sequential steps trace our exact journey.

Every image and CAD model originates directly from our Ho Chi Minh City workshop: real progress, free from artificial renders.

01

Beginning with a side-view layout rather than a sketch

The initial focus is on the overall package rather than bodywork. Key packaging elements include the battery position, its height, the remaining flat deck space above it, and the rider's weight distribution across both wheels.

All visible elements of the completed motorcycle flow directly from this initial layout. The continuous top line spanning from headstock to tail is not an aesthetic choice; it results from centering and lowering the load while eliminating frame bends designed for non-existent fuel tanks.

Developing in full-vehicle CAD from the outset allows modifications in the battery bay to immediately sync with seat height, rack positioning, and swingarm clearance, ensuring all components advance together instead of being reconciled later.

02

The frame is built before anything is styled

Stripped of all panels, the U1 reveals its underlying architecture: a tube frame, a flat plate battery bay, and a series of bolt-on mounting tabs for attaching racks and covers.

We deliberately construct the bare frame first and test it unclad. A frame that relies on bodywork to look right is merely masking underlying flaws. On this bike, every weld and tab remains fully exposed, precisely as a neighborhood mechanic will encounter it years down the line.

These mounting tabs serve a critical purpose. Because the front rack, mid rails, rear deck, and side covers attach directly to this core frame rather than to one another, fleet operators can reconfigure components without compromising the primary load path.

03

The first removable battery placement and enclosure

The dual removable battery packs are intentionally positioned low, centrally balanced, and between the rider's knees within the frame's upper section, ahead of the seat, to conserve space, remain concealed, and avoid tail-mounted handling issues.

Placement was prioritized for ergonomics so that batteries can be swapped tool-free while standing in traffic or rain. Height, reach angle, and aperture size were engineered around rider needs.

Built for Vietnamese road conditions, the protective enclosure transfers load directly to the frame. A hinged lid allows sub-two-minute swaps, and single or dual-battery setups only alter the bay's contents, leaving the vehicle unchanged.

04

Every part gets a number before it gets a shape

Assigning individual part numbers and revision histories to every component during prototyping transforms a simple design concept into a specification suppliers can reliably price.

Providing distinct part records rather than basic visual representations enables precise re-quoting, ensuring cost-reduction adjustments remain tied to specific revisions.

This systematic approach validates the bill of materials, preventing cost engineering from becoming speculative estimation.

Revision controlBOM disciplineVendor ready
05

Tooling is designed at the same time as the part

Structural ribs, draft angles, and mounting bosses are included in the primary CAD revision, ensuring parts release from molds without requiring retroactive modifications.

Integrating production requirements early on prevents downstream performance penalties, whereas resolving manufacturing constraints late in development leads to heavier parts and costly design fixes.

By preventing redundant expenditures, this strategy protects capital. Since tooling is the largest initial investment before manufacturing, modifying a part after machining the steel dies forces operators to buy a new mold.

06

Testing a physical panel on a fabricated frame reveals real-world fit

While CAD models show theoretical fit, only a welded frame subject to real-world tolerances proves whether a panel fits today.

We check the first-off side cover directly against the frame to evaluate top-edge gaps and confirm fastener alignment. Unpolished and candid, these photographs reflect our real development progress, which is why we publish them instead of renders.

We aim for the side cover to mount and detach using just two fasteners without manual adjustment. Easy removal is essential, as the flat cover supports branding wraps while keeping rider maintenance simple.

07

Prototype 01, running and being ridden

This shows a functional U1 prototype with raw panels, in the workshop, actively undergoing test rides and measurements rather than staged for promotional photography.

The steel frame remains unfinished, and the white bodywork deliberately unpainted. Avoiding digital renders, we present the motorcycle in this raw state so customers do not have to rely on faith. Every part will undergo at least one additional design iteration before tooling is committed.

Our next phases prioritize execution over cosmetic changes: progressing from bill-of-materials cost reduction to full whole-vehicle type approval with the Vietnam Register, then moving directly to customer deliveries.

Where It Stands

What is done, and what is not.

The U1 is a running prototype in ride testing. The frame, package layout, and electronics architecture are settled. Panels, brackets, and the bill of materials are still moving.

Production tooling has not been ordered. Whole-vehicle type approval has not been granted by the Vietnam Register, and no vehicle can be delivered to a customer until it is. Specifications on this site describe a vehicle under development and will change.
