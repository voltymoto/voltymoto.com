Volty / Design

Every part earns its place.

The U1 was not styled and then engineered. It was packaged, welded, wired, numbered, tooled, and ridden, in that order. This is that process, shown in the state it is actually in.

How We Decide

Four rules, held against a real vehicle.

These are the four commitments the company is built on. Below is what each one actually decided on this bike, including the things it removed.

Rule 01

Design with purpose

Applied here as a veto, not a slogan.

On the U1 this rule kills things. It killed a fairing, a body-colored battery cover, and a second instrument display. Each would have added cost and a part number without adding a kilometer of range, a kilogram of payload, or a minute of uptime. What survived the veto is what you see in the exploded view: frame, bay, deck, covers, racks.

Rule 02

Focus on a need

One rider, drawn to scale, sits behind every dimension.

The package is dimensioned around a working rider at the end of a twelve hour shift, not a showroom average. Seat height, bar reach, deck height, and the weight of a loaded rear rack were all set against that one person. When two options were both defensible, we picked the one that was better at hour eleven rather than the one that looked better at zero.

Rule 03

Solve a specific problem

Named problems, each traceable to a part on the bike.

Fuel cost that moves with the pump became a swappable pack. Diagnostic time that eats a shift became a hinged bay reachable in a minute. Dealer dependency became bolt-on panels and standard fasteners a corner-shop mechanic already owns. Every one of those is a specific complaint from a specific rider, answered by a specific component.

Rule 04

Be meaningful

The test we apply after the engineering is finished.

A vehicle earns meaning when it changes a rider's economics, and keeps it when they still choose to ride it on a day nobody is paying them. That second half is why the U1 has a real silhouette, proper suspension travel, and a seat two people can use. Strip those and we would have built an appliance that pays for itself and gets sold the moment something better arrives.

The four rules come from the Vision. This page is the record of what happened when we held a real vehicle against them.

The Process

Seven steps, in the order they happened.

Photographs and CAD from the Ho Chi Minh City workshop. Nothing here is a render dressed up as progress.

01

It starts as a side view, not a sketch

The first thing drawn is not the bodywork. It is the package: where the battery sits, how low it sits, how much flat deck is left above it, and where the rider's weight lands relative to both wheels.

Everything visible on the finished bike is downstream of this drawing. The straight top line from headstock to tail is not a styling decision, it is what you get when you insist the load sits low and centered and refuse to bend the frame around a fuel tank that does not exist.

Working in full-vehicle CAD from day one means a change to the battery bay immediately shows up as a change to the seat height, the rack position, and the swingarm clearance. Nothing gets designed in isolation and reconciled later.

02

The frame is built before anything is styled

This is the U1 with every panel removed. Tube frame, flat plate battery bay, and a row of bolt-on tabs where the racks and covers will attach.

We build the structure first and live with it bare for a while, because a frame that only looks right once it is covered is a frame with a problem hiding in it. You can see every weld and every tab on this bike, which is exactly how a mechanic in a corner shop will see it in three years.

The tabs matter more than they look. Front rack, mid rails, rear deck, and side covers all mount to this structure rather than to each other, so a fleet can change a configuration without touching the load path.

03

Packaging the electronics where a hand can reach them

Electric two wheelers tend to bury the controller somewhere that requires half the bike to come apart. We put the electronics in a sealed bay under the seat with a hinged lid, reachable in under a minute with no special tool.

The reason is economic rather than aesthetic. A bike in a workshop earns nothing. Every hour of diagnostic time is an hour of a rider's income, and on a fleet it is an hour of utilization across hundreds of units.

Drawing it this way costs volume. The bay eats storage space we could have sold as a feature. We took the trade because a fleet operator asks about downtime before they ask about litres.

04

Every part gets a number before it gets a shape

Each component in the model carries its own part number and revision. It looks like bureaucracy on a prototype. It is the difference between a design study and something a supplier can actually quote against.

When we go to a vendor for a re-quote, we are handing over an identified part with a revision history, not a picture. When a cost-down changes a bracket, the change is traceable to a revision rather than a conversation someone half remembers.

This discipline is what makes a bill of materials real. Without it, cost engineering is guesswork, and every price we publish would be a hope rather than a number.

05

Tooling is designed at the same time as the part

Ribs, draft angles, and fastener bosses are in the model from the first revision, not added once someone discovers the part cannot be released from a mold.

A part redesigned late for manufacturability is a part that gets worse: thicker, heavier, and more expensive, because the fix has to work around decisions already frozen elsewhere. Designing to the process from the start keeps the compromise small.

It also protects the money. Tooling is the single largest line item before delivery, and a tool cut for a part that then changes is a tool paid for twice.

06

The first panel on a real frame tells the truth

A CAD model will tell you a panel fits. A welded frame with tolerance in it will tell you whether it fits on this bike, today.

This is a first-off side cover held against the frame to check fastener positions and the gap along the top edge. It is not pretty and it is not meant to be. Photographs like this are the honest record of where a design is, which is why we would rather publish them than wait for a render.

What we are looking for here is whether the cover can come off and go back on with two fasteners and no adjustment, because the flat side panel is a surface a corporate customer will want to wrap, and a rider will need to remove.

07

Prototype 01, running and being ridden

This is where the U1 is now: a running prototype with unfinished panels, in the workshop, being ridden and measured rather than photographed for a brochure.

The white bodywork is unpainted. The frame is unfinished steel. Every part on it will change at least once more before tooling is committed. We are showing it in this state deliberately, because a company that only shows renders is a company asking you to take its word for it.

What happens next is not styling. It is a cost-down across the bill of materials, then whole-vehicle type approval with the Vietnam Register, then customer deliveries. In that order, with no shortcuts available.

Where It Stands

What is done, and what is not.

The U1 is a running prototype in ride testing. The frame, package layout, and electronics architecture are settled. Panels, brackets, and the bill of materials are still moving.

Production tooling has not been ordered. Whole-vehicle type approval has not been granted by the Vietnam Register, and no vehicle can be delivered to a customer until it is. Specifications on this site describe a vehicle under development and will change.
