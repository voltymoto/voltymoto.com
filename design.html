Our Design / How the U1 was built

We started with the load, not the look

Every component of the U1 was designed in direct response to rider feedback. Here is the sequence of our process and the changes made at each stage.

The process

Six steps, in order

Large-scale design follows a structured order rather than a linear path. Bypassing any step inevitably leads to costly repercussions later during tooling, where modifications become significantly more expensive.

01

Define the load case

Prior to sketching a single line, we defined the bike's survival requirements: carrying 200 kg, enduring hundreds of loaded pull-aways daily, navigating unpaved roads, and handling riders paid per trip who won't baby the machine. These core requirements dictated the frame design, rather than the frame dictating the specs.

Every subsequent design decision stems directly from this document, including the wheelbase, rack dimensions, and weight distribution relative to the rear axle.

02

Break it into parts that can be made here

Relying on a single factory worldwide for a component risks halting production. To prevent this, assemblies are broken down so that individual pieces align with the existing capabilities of Vietnamese suppliers, such as tube bending, sheet-metal laser cutting and folding, and basic injection molding.

This approach also minimizes repair expenses after a crash. Instead of replacing a costly single-molded body panel, dividing it into four separate components allows riders to replace only the damaged piece.

03

Put the electronics where a mechanic can reach them

Instead of relying on a dealer network, fleet vehicles are serviced by the nearest technician. To ensure diagnosis doesn't require removing bodywork, the controller, wiring, and connectors are all housed in a single tray beneath a hinged lid.

Designing a single high-quality compartment proved more cost-effective than building a separate service hatch and glovebox, allowing this tray to serve as dry storage for the rider as well.

04

Print it before you tool it

Before placing any tooling order, every bracket, tray, and cover is 3D printed and test-fitted on the physical frame. While printing a component takes only an afternoon, identifying a flaw after cutting the mould ruins the entire tool.

Physical prototyping is equally vital for refining ergonomic feel; determining whether a latch can be operated while wearing gloves is a practical test that CAD alone cannot resolve.

05

Weld a frame and load it

In-house fabrication allows us to bench-test each frame at its rated payload prior to operation. Rather than relying on model assumptions, we verify the welds, mounting bosses, and rack interfaces under actual load conditions.

While building frames in-house takes longer than purchasing off-the-shelf alternatives, it offers the flexibility to adapt and modify the design within a week when updated load cases require it.

06

Ride it before it looks finished

Long before the prototype looks good in photographs, it is ridden with unpainted bodywork mounted to a welded frame. Real-world riding remains the only way to discover the issues a rider will notice after day two.

The central principle of this page is that styling must come last, layered over a functional and proven package.

What we hold to

Three non-negotiable rules we set

01

Prioritize serviceability over sealed units

While sealed components appear refined, they present critical failures in remote environments. We utilize standard fasteners and accessible panels, ensuring that any wear item is reachable with basic tools rather than a professional workshop lift.

02

Utilize modular panel design

Segmenting the bodywork ensures that localized abrasions require replacing only a single component rather than an entire fairing. Within a fleet environment, surface wear is a persistent reality; we treat these repairs as predictable maintenance costs rather than exceptional incidents.

03

Source materials where they are assembled

Specifying components based on existing regional manufacturing processes allows us to utilize domestic suppliers effectively. Local sourcing minimizes shipping delays and enables rapid design iterations, ensuring that technical adjustments remain close to the production line rather than traversing international borders.

Where it leads

One platform, diverse applications

Integrating mounting points directly into the frame, rather than appending them as an afterthought, enables the chassis to accommodate varied bodywork configurations. These iterations represent functional design studies rather than a definitive product lineup; consequently, only specific configurations will be available for the initial release.

Current status and development

The U1 remains a functional prototype undergoing rigorous technical validation. Every visual representation on this page utilizes our proprietary CAD models, additive components, and welded chassis. These running prototypes are documented in their natural state, serving as design studies rather than final production units. No unit can be delivered before whole-vehicle type approval from the Vietnam Register.
