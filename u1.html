Our design

We started with the load, not the look.

Before a single line was sketched, we wrote down what the bike has to survive: 200 kg, hundreds of loaded pull-aways a day, broken roads, and riders paid by the trip. The frame follows from that.

  • 01
    Define the load case

    The load and the working day set the wheelbase, the rack sizes and the weight over the rear axle.

  • 02
    Make it here

    Parts are split to match what Vietnamese suppliers already make, so no single factory can stop the line.

  • 03
    Keep it fixable

    Electronics sit in one tray under a hinged lid, and bodywork is split into panels you replace one at a time.

  • 04
    Prove it, then tool it

    Every part is test-fitted and every frame load-tested before a mould is cut. Styling comes last.

Battery and charging

Fifty-four seconds, not a fuel queue.

Swap one pack or both at a cabinet, or carry a pack inside and charge it overnight. Pick a scenario and run it.

0:00Ready
54 sOne pack at a cabinet
1:58Both packs at a cabinet
2–3 hPer pack at home, no wallbox
2.2 kWhPer pack, one or two fitted

Swap times are design targets for the U1 bay. Home charging uses a floor dock on a standard household outlet. Selex is our current swap partner, and the bay is engineered to accept packs from more than one provider; further networks are named here only once agreements are signed.

What you are riding

Built to be fixed, loaded, and lived with.

Running gear

Parts a street mechanic already stocks

Chain drive, twin rear shocks, a conventional swingarm and disc brakes at both ends. Nothing exotic, so no repair waits on one supplier.

Load path

The racks are structure, not accessories

Front deck, mid rails and rear deck each bolt to the frame on their own. The 200 kg rating is a frame number, not a rack number.

Cockpit

Everything you touch, and nothing else

Wide bars, a flat seat and one instrument pod, set for hour eleven of a long day. No app needed to ride. Two up with the rear seat fitted.
